Ayame took a slow breath as she sat with the cup of tea in her hands, still staring at the steam rising faintly from it. After a moment of silence, she finally looked up at the granny and asked softly, “If that’s okay… could you please tell me more about Akio?”
The old woman nodded, her eyes growing distant as if she were stepping back into time.
“At first, when I took the brothers in,” she began gently, “Akio had gone completely silent. After the tragedy and the cold shoulders from the relatives, it was as though his voice had vanished. He refused to speak even a word. Every day, he would take his little brother in his arms and go to the park nearby. I think that must have been the same park where their parents once took them to play. He would sit there for hours, just… lost.”
Ayame tightened her grip on the teacup, her heart sinking at the thought of a little boy sitting in silence with such a heavy weight inside.
“But slowly,” the granny continued, “I managed to make my way into his heart. With time, he began to open up, little by little. He started helping me with chores, carrying his brother along wherever he went. I often saw him holding the baby with such care while trying to sweep the yard or fetch water.”
She gave a faint, bittersweet smile. “The neighbors noticed, too. They would call him over for help, small things like carrying baskets or running errands. And in return, they’d give him treats. I could see he enjoyed it, not for the sweets, but for the feeling of being needed. That boy… he loved to help people. It gave him joy.”
Ayame’s eyes softened. She could picture it clearly—Akio, a little boy with his brother in his arms, smiling shyly as he helped those around him.
“And around that time,” the granny said with a twinkle in her tired eyes, “he started talking about you. How smart you were in your studies. The speech you gave once at school. He remembered the smallest things. You inspired him, Ayame. Truly, you did.”
Ayame froze for a moment, her chest tightening as a flood of emotions rose within her. She hadn’t known she ever mattered to him in such a way.
Just then, a small face peeked shyly from behind the doorframe. It was the little boy.
The granny noticed and called gently, “Souta, come here.”
The boy hesitated, then slowly walked forward, clutching the edge of his shirt.
“Go on,” the granny urged him softly. “Say sorry to her.”
Ayame quickly shook her head, placing her cup down and smiling warmly. “No, no, there’s no need for that. It’s totally fine.”
But the boy bowed his head and whispered, “I’m sorry…” before glancing up with wide, apologetic eyes.
Ayame’s smile deepened, though her heart ached at the sight of him. “Really, there’s no need. You’ve done nothing wrong.”
The granny placed a gentle hand on Souta’s shoulder and nodded in gratitude.
Ayame then stood slowly, placing her empty cup on the table. “Thank you… for sharing all this with me. I should get going now.”
The granny gave her a knowing look, one that held both sorrow and warmth. “You’re always welcome here, Ayame.”
Ayame bowed her head slightly and stepped out of the house, her mind swirling with everything she had just heard, and with the image of Akio as both a boy burdened with grief and a boy filled with kindness.
