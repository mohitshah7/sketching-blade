The old woman gestured gently, “Come, child, sit down.”
Ayame lowered herself onto the cushion as the granny brought her a cup of steaming tea. The warmth rose into the air, but Ayame hardly noticed. She wrapped her hands around the cup, her eyes fixed on the wooden floor. Her mind was tangled in disbelief, unsure how to act or what to say.
Ayame’s hands trembled slightly as she held the cup of tea, its warmth not reaching the cold confusion running through her veins. She sat at the edge of the wooden chair in the quiet room, her gaze fixed on the floorboards, unable to meet the old woman’s eyes. The faint aroma of green tea filled the space, but Ayame could barely register it. Her mind was storming with questions that had no answers.
The granny placed her own cup down with a soft clink and looked at Ayame with a mixture of patience and sorrow.
“Child,” she said gently, “I believe you. If you say you met him… then it must be so. Spirits don’t always rest when the heart still has something left behind.”
Ayame’s eyes lifted slightly, her lips parting as if to speak, but her disbelief held her tongue. Finally, she managed, her voice low and unsteady:
“But… how is this possible? He… he helped me. He spoke to me as if he was alive. I—I didn’t even know him before. Why me? Why would he appear before me?”
The granny’s face softened, and for the first time, a faint, knowing smile tugged at her lips.
“Ah, but you are wrong. He knew you, Ayame. He knew you very well.”
Ayame blinked, stunned. “Knew… me?”
The old woman leaned back, her eyes clouding with memory. “When Akio was younger, back in his middle school days, he often spoke about a girl in his class. He said she was quiet yet strong… thoughtful, kind, but always misunderstood by others. He admired her spirit. He would come home and talk about how she walked, how she answered questions, even how she stared out of the window when no one noticed. He was… fascinated by her. Inspired, even. He said he wanted to be more like her.”
Ayame’s grip on the teacup tightened, her breath caught in her throat. “Me…? You mean… all this time…”
The granny nodded slowly, her voice steady but heavy.
“Yes, child. That girl was you. Akio never said your name often, but I remember. It was always you.”
The silence that followed was thick with the weight of revelation. Ayame’s chest ached—confusion tangled with a sorrow she didn’t know how to carry.
The old woman took a long breath, her eyes drifting to the framed photograph of Akio on the wall. “But Akio’s story was never easy. He was only a child when tragedy came knocking. His father died suddenly… in a terrible accident. The shock of it broke his poor mother’s heart, and that very night, she passed away as well, leaving little Akio and his infant brother behind. Alone. Helpless.”
Ayame’s eyes widened. Her hands trembled harder, spilling a few drops of tea onto her lap, but she didn’t notice.
The granny’s voice grew heavier, her face tightening with suppressed bitterness.
“All their so-called relatives, those heartless people, turned their backs. None of them wanted to take in two boys who had lost everything. I was just an old woman back then… but when I heard of it, I couldn’t stand aside. I wasn’t their real grandmother, no… but a friend of their late grandfather. That bond, and my conscience, pushed me to step in. I took them into this house. Raised them as best as I could, though my hands were already tired with age.”
Her eyes glistened, though her tone remained steady. “Akio carried so much weight for a child. He grew up far too quickly, protecting his brother, hiding his grief. But even in those shadows, he would talk about you, Ayame… as if thinking of you gave him strength.”
Ayame lowered her eyes, tears slipping silently down her cheeks. She didn’t know whether to feel grateful, guilty, or broken. She had never spoken to Akio back then—at least, not in a way that mattered. She had no idea someone had been watching her, finding light in her presence while she herself had felt invisible to the world.
She finally whispered, her voice cracking, “All this time… he carried so much, and I never knew.”
The granny placed her weathered hand gently over Ayame’s. “And yet, perhaps… he wanted you to know now. That even from beyond, he still wished to help you, just as you unknowingly once helped him.”
Ayame’s cup rattled softly against the saucer. She couldn’t find words. The tea had gone cold, but the story burned inside her chest.
