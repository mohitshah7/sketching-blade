When Ayame returned home that evening, Akio’s words lingered in her mind like a quiet lantern lighting her steps. She remembered the way he had spoken, not harshly, but firmly, about her fears, her hesitations, and the weight she carried unnecessarily.

That night, for the first time in months, she didn’t sit in her room sulking or replaying arguments. Instead, she took a deep breath and approached her parents. She apologized where her pride once refused, explained herself when silence used to choke her, and listened, truly listened, to what they had to say. The arguments that once ended in slammed doors and tears softened into conversation. It wasn’t perfect, but something inside her had shifted: she had decided to meet people halfway instead of hiding behind walls.

With her siblings, she practiced patience. When her younger brother teased her, she smiled instead of snapping back. When her sister mocked her choice of dress, Ayame only shrugged and said, “I like it.” The sting was still there, but Akio’s words echoed, Not every blow deserves your blood. Slowly, the house that had always felt like a battlefield began to quiet down.

Two days passed, and Ayame felt lighter. The world hadn’t changed overnight, but she had. And somehow, that was enough.

On the third morning, as she walked through the market, she saw the cabbage seller arranging his produce. Before she could even turn away, he spotted her. For a moment she braced herself, expecting another rude remark. But instead, the man lowered his head.

“I was wrong that day,” he muttered. “You’re a good girl. Forgive me.”

Ayame blinked in surprise. She hadn’t demanded it, hadn’t fought for it, yet the apology came. She felt something unclench inside her chest. She bowed slightly and replied, “Thank you. I forgive you.” And she meant it.

Later, unable to shake her curiosity, she asked a friend about the stranger who had changed everything.

“Flawlee?” her friend repeated. “Oh, you mean Akio. He went to our middle school, didn’t you know? He lives near the old maple road.”

Heart beating fast, Ayame followed the directions until she found herself at a quiet, worn-down house with a small garden. She hesitated before knocking, but finally raised her hand and tapped at the door.

It opened a crack, and a young boy, no more than ten, peered out with sharp, suspicious eyes.

“Who are you?” he demanded.

“Um… excuse me,” Ayame said softly. “Does Akio live here?”

The boy’s face changed instantly, his small fists clenching. “What business do you have with him?” he snapped. Before she could answer, he turned and bolted down the hallway, his footsteps echoing with anger.

Confused, Ayame stood frozen until an elderly woman appeared at the doorway, her arms heavy with bags from the market. She smiled kindly despite the weariness in her face.

“Ah, forgive my grandson,” the woman said gently. “Please, come in.”

Inside, the house smelled of tatami and simmering miso. Ayame hesitated before asking, “That boy… is he Akio’s younger brother?”

The old woman set down her bags, then gave a slow, sad nod. “Yes. He is.”

Ayame lowered her gaze, her heart twisting. “Did I… did I do something wrong? Why was he so angry with me?”

The grandmother sighed, her eyes drifting toward the far wall. “It is not you, child. He is just… protective.”

Ayame followed her gaze, and that was when she saw it. A framed photograph hung on the wall, Akio, his hair black as night, his smile faint but kind. Around the photo were white flowers, the frame draped with a small ribbon of mourning.

Ayame’s breath caught in her throat.

It was a memorial. Akio, was no longer alive.

And yet, he had stood before her not long ago, guiding her, changing the course of her days.
