The sun leaned toward the horizon as Ayame and the boy walked side by side, their footsteps slow and unhurried. The air between them carried a silence that was strangely comfortable, until he finally spoke.

“You seem down,” he said gently. “Not your mood… deeper than that. It feels like your mind is tangled in invisible strings, and they’re pulling you down.”

Ayame gave a sharp laugh. “It’s nothing like that. I’m fine. It’s just today’s incident. That vendor, without even caring what I’ve been going through, he added another mess to my life. I despise people like that.”

The boy hesitated, then asked softly, “May I say something? If it’s okay with you.”

His voice, there was something about it. It wasn’t just pleasant; it was compelling. Each word seemed to tug at her, inviting her to speak. Why does it feel like I must answer him? Why does it feel like opening up to him is… necessary?

He caught her silence and smiled modestly. “It seems I must be boring you. Sorry for that.”

“No,” Ayame said quickly. “Please. Say whatever you feel like.”

He chuckled. “Then let me start by saying, you’ve already admitted something is draining you.”

Her lips curved faintly. “I realized that.”

“Then tell me,” he urged gently. “What is it?”

She exhaled slowly. “It’s everyone around me… everyone close to me. You probably know I was popular at school. But it backfired. People talked to me, but not genuinely. Underneath, they despised me for being better. Hated me for excelling. That isn’t fair, is it? I didn’t have natural gifts, I worked for everything.”

He leaned closer as if to respond, but she interrupted, her voice pleading. “Please, let me finish. I don’t think I’ll be able to open up like this again.”

He nodded. “Then I’ll listen.”

She looked away, her eyes tracing the dust on the road. “That’s why I stopped pushing myself. I started blending in. I had one true friend. But now… she has someone else. She divides her time between us. I thought distancing myself would make it easier for her, but instead, I may have pushed her away. I think I hurt her.”

The boy’s gaze softened. “Why don’t you talk to your family about this?”

“My family?” Ayame let out a humorless laugh. “They don’t understand me. My father keeps me under rules and restrictions. I love him, but I feel suppressed. I even shouted at him once, and that wasn’t me. I hurt him, too. My mother… sometimes she’s rude even when it isn’t my fault. And my sister, she’s smart, but she always finds ways to pick fights. We used to be close, but now we’re in different worlds.

“It feels like there’s no one left to turn to. Like I’m hurting everyone around me. That’s why… when you saved me, it meant more than you can imagine. Thank you.”

The boy’s voice deepened, steady as stone. “Then think carefully about this, why don’t you want to hurt them? Because they matter to you. That’s the truth. But you must understand, it’s impossible to live without hurting someone. By simply existing, we hurt others. By getting involved, or by not getting involved. What matters is self-awareness. The fact that you worry about hurting others means you care for them. That’s the price of caring, you must be prepared to hurt, even when you don’t want to.”

He tilted his head. “Tell me, are traffic rules important?”

“Of course,” she answered.

“But you see them as chains. Why follow something that takes your freedom?”

Ayame stopped walking, glaring at him. “Are you crazy? Rules save lives. Without them, there’d be chaos every second. Even my sister knows this!”

He smiled knowingly. “Then why not see your father the same way? He is your government. His rules are meant to protect you. Every restriction is born from a mistake he’s already seen in life. Don’t you think it hurts him to be strict? But he does it to save you.

“And your mother, she spends her days exhausted. Don’t you think her harshness might come from fatigue, or even worry? That’s not cruelty, Ayame. That’s love, disguised.”

Ayame’s lips curved into a faint smile. “That all sounds so hard…”

“It is,” he admitted, grinning. “But you can do it. I did. And your sister, she’s not your enemy. She’s seeking your attention. I know because my younger brother did the same. To them, growing distance feels like being ignored.”

Ayame blinked, surprised by how much sense it made. His words are like a sword and a shield. They cut, but they also protect. He’s so mature… even though we’re the same age. Pain must have shaped him this way. Yes… there’s a saying: we mature through pain, not days.

The boy looked at her with calm certainty. “No wound is deep enough to break you. It’s your own salt, your own presumptions, that make the cut sting. Worry less. Enjoy more. You never know when you’re gonna—”

“Gonna what?” Ayame asked.

He only smiled, stepping back. “It’s evening. Time for me to go. Goodbye.”

And just like that, he turned and ran.

“Wait!” she cried. “At least tell me your name! What about my friend?”

His voice echoed faintly as he disappeared into the distance: “Ask anyone from our batch about Flalee. They’ll tell you my name. Add that third person to your group.”

Her eyes widened. “Flalee? Wait, you’re the Flying Leopard? The athlete?!”

But the boy was already gone.
